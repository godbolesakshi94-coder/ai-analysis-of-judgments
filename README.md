# ai-analysis-of-judgments
1. Introduction
This project examines how the Supreme Court of India has addressed professional ethics, advocates’ duties, accountability in legal practice, and emerging concerns created by the use of artificial intelligence (AI) in the legal system.
Traditionally, legal ethics has focused on duties such as honesty towards the court, loyalty and confidentiality towards clients, professional competence, proper conduct during hearings, and compliance with disciplinary rules. The increasing use of AI creates additional concerns. For example, lawyers and courts may encounter AI-generated legal authorities that do not exist, inaccurate legal research, fabricated citations, or electronic material that may be manipulated through deepfake technology.
The project therefore studies the connection between established professional duties and newer forms of accountability arising from AI-assisted legal work. It uses Supreme Court judgments as the primary unit of analysis.

2. Aim and Objectives
The main aim is to understand the evolution of legal ethics in India in the context of artificial intelligence and changing legal practice.
The objectives are:
1. To identify the professional duties and ethical responsibilities expected of advocates.
2. To examine how the Supreme Court deals with professional misconduct, discipline, and accountability.
3. To study judicial concerns relating to AI-generated or non-existent judgments and legal citations.
4. To examine the reliability and verification of electronic evidence in an era of AI and deepfakes.
5. To identify continuities between traditional professional ethics and newer expectations of responsible AI use.
6. To organise the judgments into a structured dataset and use charts to identify broad patterns.
   
3. Research Questions
- How has the Supreme Court interpreted advocates’ professional duties and misconduct during the study period?
- What standards of honesty, diligence, decorum, and accountability emerge from the selected judgments?
- How have courts responded to the use of AI-generated or fabricated legal authorities?
- What concerns arise regarding electronic evidence, authenticity, and deepfakes?
- Do the selected decisions indicate that traditional professional duties also apply when lawyers use AI tools?
  
4. Scope of the Study
The dataset contains 20 Supreme Court of India judgments or judicial matters with decision dates falling between 1 September 2021 and 31 August 2026.
The cases cover themes including:
- Advocates’ professional misconduct and disciplinary proceedings.
- Duties of advocates and Advocates-on-Record.
- Courtroom conduct, decorum, and the proper discharge of professional responsibilities.
- Enrolment and designation of Senior Advocates.
- The relationship between advocates and clients, including professional accountability.
- AI-generated or non-existent legal precedents and citations.
- Electronic evidence, authenticity, and safeguards relevant to AI-generated or manipulated material.
The study is limited to the cases selected and recorded in the spreadsheet. It is not a complete survey of every Supreme Court decision on legal ethics or AI during the period.

5. Methodology
This is a descriptive, judgment-based content analysis.
5.1 Selection of judgments
The project uses 20 selected Supreme Court matters decided within the stated five-year period. The cases were selected for their relevance to professional ethics, advocate accountability, court procedure, AI-related legal research concerns, or the reliability of electronic evidence.
5.2 Unit of analysis
Each row in the Data sheet represents one judgment or judicial matter. The made-up example in row 5 of the spreadsheet is only a formatting example and is not included in the 20-case dataset.
5.3 Data fields
The spreadsheet records:
- Case name.
- Citation or case number.
- Date of decision and year.
- Bench strength.
- Type of case.
- Who brought the case.
- Outcome and who won.
- Main legal provision.
- Legal issue in one line.
- Short summary of the decision.
- Related case or precedent, where identified.
- Source link.
These fields make it possible to compare the cases using common categories rather than relying only on narrative summaries.
5.4 Analysis
The Chart counts sheet is designed to generate charts showing:
1. Number of judgments by year.
2. Number of judgments by outcome.
3. Number of judgments by the category of party who brought the case.
4. Number of judgments by type of case.
5. Number of judgments by the category of party who won.
The charts are descriptive. They show the distribution of the selected cases and should not be treated as proof that one category is more important or more successful across all Supreme Court litigation.
